# Versioning Space: Capturing How Concepts, Languages, and Locations Change

<Callout type="info">
This article is part of the **Foundations** series, exploring the fundamental abstractions that power Rendered Useful. Here we examine how Space, manifested through Concepts, Languages, and Locations, evolves over time.
</Callout>

## The River You Cannot Step Into Twice

When Heraclitus observed that you cannot step into the same river twice, he captured something profound about the nature of reality. The water flowing past your feet in this moment is not the water that was there a moment ago. The riverbed shifts. Even the person stepping in has changed.

We tend to treat space (physical places, abstract concepts, systems of language) as if they were fixed coordinates on a map. "Berlin." "Privacy." "English." But these are not stable points. They are rivers.

**Berlin in 1985** was a divided city, a symbol of Cold War tensions, a place where families were separated by concrete and ideology. **Berlin in 2025** is a reunified European capital, a startup hub, a cultural melting pot. They share a name and a location on a map, but they are fundamentally different *places* in the sense that matters to human experience.

The same is true for concepts. When your grandmother spoke of "privacy," she meant closed doors, sealed letters, the right to whispered conversations. When your teenager speaks of "privacy," they navigate consent dialogs, data brokers, algorithmic inference, and the strange intimacy of being known by machines that have never met them.

Same word. Different concept entirely.

## Why This Matters for Knowledge

Most systems that organize knowledge treat these categories as eternal. A tag is a tag. A location is a location. An article written in 2010 about "privacy" sits alongside an article from 2025, as if they were discussing the same thing.

But they aren't.

This creates a subtle form of confusion. A reader encounters outdated advice presented as current wisdom. Historical context collapses into a flattened present. The evolution of ideas becomes invisible.

I want to try something different. I want to model reality more honestly, to acknowledge that **knowledge is contextual, and context changes**.

## The Three Rivers

In our framework, Space manifests through three dimensions, and each one flows:

### Concepts: Ideas That Evolve

Consider how "artificial intelligence" has transformed:

| Era | What "AI" Meant |
|-----|-----------------|
| 1960s | Expert systems, symbolic reasoning, the promise of thinking machines |
| 1990s | Neural networks (first wave), fuzzy logic, narrow automation |
| 2010s | Machine learning, deep learning, pattern recognition at scale |
| 2020s | Foundation models, emergent capabilities, the blur between tool and collaborator |

An article about "AI safety" from 2015 is discussing something genuinely different than one from 2025. The concerns shifted from "will autonomous weapons make wrong decisions" to "will systems that can write and reason reshape society before we understand them."

Other concepts undergo similar transformations:

- **"Work"** evolves from industrial labor → knowledge work → remote work → AI-augmented collaboration
- **"Community"** shifts from geographic proximity → shared interests → online spaces → algorithmic curation
- **"Identity"** moves from family and nation → self-construction → digital personas → distributed selves across platforms

These aren't just semantic shifts. They represent genuine changes in how humans relate to fundamental aspects of existence.

### Languages: Living Systems

You might wonder: is language really *spatial*? We don't walk through a language the way we walk through a city.

But consider what language does. What can be said. What can be thought. What connections can be made between ideas. The vocabulary, grammar, and idioms of a language form a terrain that speakers navigate. Some paths are well-worn. Others are barely visible. Some destinations are unreachable from certain starting points. To me these are very much spatial metaphors.

When we say someone is "fluent," we mean they move freely through that space. When we say an idea is "lost in translation," we mean the path from one linguistic space to another doesn't preserve the destination.

Languages, both natural and formal, are not frozen artifacts. They breathe, adapt, and transform across generations.

Every language carries its history. Words accumulate meanings, shed old ones, borrow from neighbors. The Spanish spoken by your great grandparents is not the Spanish spoken today. Japanese before and after the Meiji Restoration. Arabic across its many regional variations. Mandarin as it absorbs technical vocabulary from global discourse. Each version reflects the world its speakers inhabited.

Programming languages undergo similar evolution. The Python of 2010 is not the Python of 2026. New features change what's possible, what's idiomatic, what's considered good practice. Code that was exemplary becomes dated. Patterns that were necessary become antipatterns.

By acknowledging that languages have versions, we can:
- Recognize that every language is a living system, shaped by its speakers' experiences
- Note when content was written in a specific linguistic or technical context
- Track how languages evolve and whether referenced code or idioms are still current

### Locations: Places in Time

Places are perhaps the most visceral example of this principle.

**San Francisco** in 2015 was a boomtown, bursting with optimism, cranes on every corner, the presumed future of work being invented in converted warehouses. **San Francisco** in 2024 had empty office towers, a crisis of homelessness, and a tech industry grappling with its own contradictions. Same coordinates. Different city.

**The Web** itself has versions:

- **Web 1.0 (1991)**: Tim Berners-Lee's invention. Static pages, hyperlinks, directories. Most people read; few people wrote. GeoCities. Guestbooks. The wonder of information being *there*.
- **Web 2.0 (2004)**: The participatory web. Everyone becomes a creator. Social networks. User generated content. "The Web as Platform." The wonder shifts to connection.
- **Web 3.0 (emerging)**: Machine readable data. AI integration. The semantic web Tim Berners-Lee envisioned in 1999. The question becomes: who is this web *for*?

Content about "building for the web" from 1998 describes a different universe than content from today. By versioning the location itself, we preserve that context.

## Epistemological Humility

This approach embodies a philosophical stance and when we version our Space dimensions, we're making several acknowledgments:

1. **Our knowledge is situated.** What we write today reflects our current understanding, shaped by our cultural moment, our available tools, our particular blind spots.

2. **Meaning drifts.** The words and concepts we use will be understood differently by future readers. By marking when something was captured, we give future readers a chance to contextualize.

3. **Places are experiences, not coordinates.** What matters about a location isn't just where it is, but what it was like to be there, at that time, in that cultural configuration.

4. **Language is alive.** The systems we use to communicate, natural and formal, are constantly evolving. Pretending otherwise creates false precision.

This is not relativism. We're not saying truth doesn't exist or that all perspectives are equal. We're saying that honest knowledge management requires acknowledging the temporal dimension of understanding.

## The Ship of Theseus

The ancient paradox asks: if you replace every plank of a ship over time, is it still the same ship?

Our answer: it depends on what you need to know.

If you're asking about legal ownership or sentimental attachment, maybe the continuity of identity matters. If you're asking about the structural integrity of the vessel, you need to know which planks are original and which are new. With this system, we can support as many perspectives on the "sameness" of an entity such as this ship as needed.

By versioning Space, we provide both views:
- **Continuity**: entities share a version group, maintaining their identity across time
- **Specificity**: each version has its own record, date, and context

You can reference "The Web" as a continuous thing that evolved, or you can reference "Web 1.0" specifically when that precision matters. The system supports both perspectives because reality requires both.

## Practical Implications

What does this mean for how we organize and present knowledge?

### Temporal Integrity
When an article references a concept, language, or location, that reference is anchored in time. Future readers can understand what the author meant, not just what those words mean now.

### Historical Navigation  
Readers can explore evolution:
- "How did 'privacy' change from 2010 to 2025?"
- "What was the web like before social media?"
- "How has this programming language evolved?"

### Honest Recommendations
When someone reads dated content, the system can offer context:
- "This discusses Web 1.0 practices. See how things have evolved: [view current version](/locations/the-web)"

### Cross-Cultural Awareness
When language variants are acknowledged, we can note when content uses culture specific idioms or assumptions that may not translate globally.

## What This Makes Possible

With Time and Space both supporting temporal modeling, we open doors to:

- **Version timelines** for any dimension: visualize how "privacy" or "AI" evolved across decades
- **Historical views** of the knowledge graph: what did the landscape look like in 2015?
- **Change detection**: flag when referenced concepts have newer versions available
- **Cross-version comparison**: how does understanding of a concept differ across eras?

But more fundamentally, we've built a system that respects a basic truth about knowledge: **it exists in time, not outside it**. We are not archiving static artifacts; we are curating a living, breathing ecosystem of ideas, languages, and places that evolve just as we do.

## A Foundation, Not a Finish Line

What we've built here is infrastructure. The versioning system for Concepts, Languages, and Locations is a building block that enables features we haven't built yet.

Consider what becomes possible when space dimensions can evolve:

**Richer relationships.** Right now, an article can reference a concept. But what if concepts could reference each other? What if "machine learning" could formally relate to "statistics" and "neural networks" in ways that change over time? The versioning foundation makes these evolving relationships tractable.

**Community contributions.** When someone wants to add a new concept or update an existing one, versioning provides a natural model. The original isn't destroyed. A new version emerges. Contributors can propose how "accessibility" should be understood in 2026 without erasing how it was understood in 2015. This enables a better bridge to migrate knowledge forward.

**Localization as versioning.** A concept explained in Japanese isn't just a translation. It's a version that reflects how that idea lives in a different linguistic and cultural space. The same infrastructure that tracks temporal evolution can track cultural adaptation.

**Learning paths.** If we know how concepts evolved, we can guide learners through that evolution. "Before you understand modern state management, here's what came before and why it changed." History becomes pedagogy.

**Deprecation and succession.** When a programming pattern becomes an antipattern, we don't delete it. We version it, mark its era, and link to what replaced it. The knowledge remains accessible to those maintaining legacy systems while newcomers are guided toward current practice.

None of these features exist today and we may or may not build all of them, But they're possible as well as many other features because the foundation is in place. We've established that space has versions, that identity persists across change, and that context matters.

The river flows. We can't step into it the same way twice. But we can mark where we stepped, and when, and what the water was like on that day. That's what versioning space means.
